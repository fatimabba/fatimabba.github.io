Fatima Abba

Why can some governments tax, build and deliver, while others with the same formal authority cannot? I am a senior at Pace University, and most of my work has pursued that question in northern Nigeria: how colonial taxation shaped the state that came after it, how provincial revenue was raised and spent under colonial rule, and whether transferring oil fields from multinational to local operators changes the violence around them. I have also spent a year measuring income inequality and social mobility in the United States. Nearly all of this evidence begins in records that were never meant to be data, and turning them into something a research design can rest on is most of the job.

Research

2025 —

Colonial taxation and state capacity in northern Nigeria

Research assistant to Augustin Bergeron, Harvard University

Whether the fiscal authority of the Sokoto Caliphate, a nineteenth-century West African empire, left an imprint on how the state collects tax today. The design compares places just inside and just outside the Caliphate's edge, which means the result depends entirely on where that edge is taken to be, and the administrators, the historians and the archival maps do not agree. I run the discontinuity and matching estimates across each competing definition rather than picking one, so the reader can see which conclusions survive the disagreement and which do not.

Much of the year went into making the comparison defensible at all: a suitability surface built from 1840–1959 climate records, so that the border is not standing in for where herding happened to be viable, and classification prompts for a language-model pipeline reading 40,000 colonial documents, with a hand-coded sample behind it to measure the error rate before any of the output was treated as data.

2025 —

Colonial public finance, 1910–1946

Research assistant to Abdoulaye Ndiaye, NYU Stern

A provincial panel of revenue, population and public-goods access, built out of thirty-six years of the Nigeria Blue Books and the 1931 and 1952–53 censuses. The Blue Books were never meant to be read as a series: categories are renamed, provinces are split and merged, and a figure recorded in one year is not always the same quantity in the next. Making it a panel meant deciding, year by year, what counted as the same place and the same line item, and georeferencing the archival maps with thin-plate splines to place those provinces on modern administrative units.

Alongside it, archival work on tax revolts and riots from the Caliphate era through the colonial period. Several districts turn out to have been nominally taxed and never effectively taxed, which is not a footnote: it changes which places the design can count as treated.

2025

Measuring US income inequality

Research assistant to Veronika Dolar, Pace University

Gini and 90/10 series across fifty years of Census and IRS data, where the definition of income shifts from one vintage to the next and a jump in the series can easily be an artifact of the change rather than anything that happened to anyone. Harmonizing those measures was most of the work. The chapter also sets the US against 22 other economies on the Great Gatsby curve, where it sits at the high-inequality, low-mobility end.

Writing

April 2026

Who Owns the Oil, Who Gets the Violence?

Senior thesis · Divestment, governance and conflict in Nigeria's oil sector

Whether transferring oil fields from multinational to local operators changes conflict nearby. A staggered difference-in-differences design on 314 fields between 2006 and 2016, with a governance index built from survey data, estimated under several of the recent corrections for staggered adoption. The association holds across them. The sensitivity analysis will not carry a causal claim, so I report it as fragile rather than as a finding, which is the part of the project I learned the most from.

Presented at the Pace Seminar in Economic Theory.
